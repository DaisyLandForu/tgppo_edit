                                                                                       Abstract

                                                  In partially observable Markov decision processes, the optimal policy generally
                                                  depends on the history of observations and past actions. Asymmetric actor-critic
                                                  methods have become popular to learn such policies when additional information,
                                                  such as the true state of the environment, is available during training. The critic,
                                                  which is not needed at execution, is given access to the state. A critic conditioned on
                                                  the state alone is generally ill-defined and yields biased policy gradients. Condition-
                                                  ing on the state and the history, the history-state critic restores both. In this paper,
                                                  we show that conditioning the critic on the state and the policy’s own memory, i.e.,
                                                  the internal representation of the history through which the policy selects its actions,
                                                  is already well-defined and gives unbiased policy gradients, removing the need for
                                                  a second recurrent approximator of the history. We call it the memory-state critic.
                                                  It follows that a critic based on the policy’s memory need not backpropagate its
                                                  loss into that memory, even though the memory is a lossy encoding of the history.
                                                  We evaluate the memory-state critic in a vision-based pursuit-evasion environment
                                                  between two quadrotors across two arena types. The pursuer is the learning agent,
                                                  and the evader is sampled per episode from a fixed pool of heuristic behaviours.
                                                  The results show that the memory-state critic outperforms the history-state critic
                                                  and converges faster. In addition to being unbiased compared to the state-only
                                                  critic, it maintains a slight edge in the wall arena, where the actor’s history carries
                                                  information that the privileged state alone does not.


                                        1     Introduction

                                        In many real-world decision problems, such as robotic control problems, an agent must take actions
                                        based on noisy or incomplete observations of the underlying state of its environment. Such prob-
                                        lems are modelled as partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) [Åström, 1965].
                                        Reinforcement learning (RL) in POMDPs addresses such decision problems through trial and error
                                        to maximise a reward [Sutton et al., 1998]. A consequence of this partial observability is that the
                                        optimal control strategy at any given time usually depends on the entire history of observations
                                        and past actions. In POMDPs, the history-dependent strategy, or policy, is usually implemented
                                        with a recurrent neural network (RNN) that recurrently updates an internal state, called a hidden
                                        state, based on successive observations and actions [Ni et al., 2022]. The hidden state is trained
                                        by backpropagating the RL loss through time, not by predicting states or observations. It therefore
                                        encodes what the policy needs from the history to act. Throughout the paper, we employ state for the
                                        environment state of the POMDP and memory for the policy’s recurrent hidden state.


                                        19th European Workshop on Reinforcement Learning (EWRL 2026).
Many successful RL algorithms for POMDPs adopt the asymmetric learning paradigm. It takes foun-
dation in the idea is that, in simulation, the true state of the environment is available at training time.
Pinto et al. [2018] introduced the asymmetric actor-critic algorithm in which the critic accesses the
state only during training, while the policy accesses the observation. This algorithm has been shown
to work better in some environments and is widely used in sim-to-real robot learning [Kaufmann
et al., 2023, Degrave et al., 2022, Vasco et al., 2024].
Baisero and Amato [2022] proved that a critic that conditions only on the state is generally ill-defined
in a POMDP. Indeed, the state alone is not a sufficient statistic of the future execution of the POMDP,
i.e., two different histories reaching the same state can induce different action distributions, since
the policy conditions on the history. Instead, the history-state critic, which takes as input both the
history and the state, is well-defined because the history and state together are a Markovian variable
of the future execution of the POMDP. In addition, the history-state critic is an unbiased estimate of
the history critic, and it can be used in the policy gradient. In practice, the critic cannot take the full
history either. It has to encode it with an encoder such as a recurrent network of its own, which is
trained by the value loss rather than the policy gradient. Having a critic that takes the history as input
has the disadvantage that it needs a second encoder of the history in comparison to the state-only
critic of Pinto et al. [2018]. It doubles the recurrent computation at training time and induces a second
network.
In this work, we introduce the memory-state critic, which conditions on the state and the policy’s
own memory. The critic takes as input the policy’s memory, but the critic loss is not backpropagated
through the recurrent encoder of the history. We prove that the memory-state critic gives the same
on-policy value function and the same unbiased policy gradient as the history-state critic. Indeed, the
memory is a sufficient statistic of the history for future actions. In the symmetric setting where the
critic does not see the state, reusing the actor’s hidden state alone would not give an unbiased gradient.
Indeed, in that setting, the memory of the agent is not a Markovian variable of the future execution
of the POMDP, since it is not necessarily a sufficient statistic for future states of the environment.
However, the combination of the state and the actor’s memory keeps the policy gradient unbiased.
We evaluate the memory-state critic on a vision-based pursuit-evasion environment between two
quadrotors. The pursuer is the learning agent. It observes the world through an onboard camera and
must catch an evader that regularly leaves the camera frame during maneuvers. The evader is sampled
per episode from a fixed pool of four heuristic behaviours, and this information is part of the critic’s
privileged state, not of the policy’s observation. The evader has a stationary policy, and the problem
reduces to a single-agent POMDP. We evaluate on two arenas that differ in their degree of partial
observability: an open arena with no obstacles and the same arena with a wall to increase the degree
of partial observability. We compare the memory-state critic to three baselines: a state-only critic, a
history-state critic, and a reactive critic that conditions on the state and the current observation and
action. The memory-state critic matches the history-state critic in the final return and converges faster,
as expected from the removal of the second recurrent encoder. It also marginally outperforms the
state-only critic in the wall arena, where the state aliases the history. We release the environment and
our implementation1 , built on IsaacLab [Mittal et al., 2023] and skrl [Serrano-Muñoz et al., 2023].
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews POMDPs and the asymmetric
policy gradient. Section 3 introduces the memory-state critic and proves its two properties. Section 4
reports the experiments, which are analysed in Section 5. The paper concludes with Section 6
discussing the related work and conclusions of our work 7.

2         Background
2.1        POMDPs and history-dependent policies

A POMDP is a tuple (S, A, O, T, O, R, P, γ). In a state st ∈ S, the agent collects observations ot ∈
O via O(ot | st ) at time t and selects actions
                                         S∞ at based on the observable history ht = (o0 , a0 , . . . , ot ).
We denote the set of histories by H = t=0 Ht with Ht ⊆ O ×(A×O)t . After taking action at ∈ A,
the state transitions according to T (st+1 | st , at ). The immediate reward is a scalar rt = R(st , at ),
the initial state s0 is drawn from P (s0 ). The discount factor γ ∈ [0, 1) weights the importance of
future rewards. A policy π(at | ht ) maps a history to a distribution over actions, and is said to be

2.2   Value functions and the asymmetric policy gradient

The canonical value function in a POMDP is the history value function V π (ht ) = Eπ [Gt | ht ],
with history-action value function Qπ (ht , at ) = Eπ [Gt | ht , at ]. The history ht is a sufficient
statistic of the future execution under π, so V π (ht ) is well-defined. Baisero and Amato [2022]
introduce the history-state value function V π (st , ht ) = Eπ [Gt | st , ht ] and the corresponding
Qπ (st , ht , at ) = Eπ [Gt | st , ht , at ], which additionally condition on the state. We therefore have
V π (ht ) = Est |ht [V π (st , ht )] and, likewise, Qπ (ht , at ) = Est |ht [Qπ (st , ht , at )].
In contrast, the state alone is not a sufficient statistic of the process: two histories reaching the same
state can induce different action distributions, since the policy conditions on the history. As a result,
the time-invariant state value function V π (s) is generally ill-defined. Even when well-defined, the
resulting policy gradient is biased [Baisero and Amato, 2022].
For a parameterised history-dependent policy πθ , Baisero and Amato [2022] showed that the policy
gradient can be written equivalently with either value function as a baseline:

2.3   Recurrent actor-critic architectures

In practice, the size of the history ht grows linearly in t which becomes impractical. The standard
approach is to encode it through a recurrent neural network. A function zt = f (ht ) is a recurrent
encoder when it admits an update rule zt = u(zt−1 , at−1 , ot ) from an initial hidden state z0 and an
arbitrary null action a0 . The encoder is deterministic given its inputs, as is standard for recurrent
networks. The policy is then parameterised as
                               πθ (at | ht ) = gθ (at | fθ (ht )) = gθ (at | zta ),                           (2)
where fθ is the recurrent encoder and gθ is an action head producing the action distribution. We write
zta = fθ (ht ) for the policy’s memory at time t.
The history-state critic of Baisero and Amato [2022] is implemented analogously, with its own
recurrent encoder ztc = fψ (ht ) on the critic side and an MLP head producing Vψ (st , ztc ). The two
encoders are trained in parallel, fθ by the policy gradient and fψ by the value loss, typically with TD
learning. Figure 1 illustrates this design. It duplicates the recurrent computation at training time, and
creates a second representation-learning problem on the critic side. The representation z c learned by
the critic may also diverge from z a learned by the policy, since the two are trained from different
losses. We show in the next section that this second encoder is not necessary.

3.2   On-policy equality

Proposition 1 (On-policy equality). Let fθ be a deterministic recurrent encoder, and πθ (a | h) =
gθ (a | fθ (h)) the corresponding recurrent policy. Then, for all reachable (ht , st ),
       V π (st , zta ) = V π (st , ht )      and         Qπ (st , zta , at ) = Qπ (st , ht , at )    with zta = fθ (ht ).    (4)

Proof. We hereafter show that conditioning on (st , zta ) and conditioning on (st , ht ) induce the
same on-policy distribution over future trajectories (st+k , at+k )k≥0 . The policy’s action satisfies
at ∼ gθ (· | zta ) by the factorisation (2), so it depends on ht only through zta . The transition
st+1 ∼ T (· | st , at ) depends on ht only through at , hence only through zta . T he next observation
ot+1 ∼ O(· | st+1 ) depends on ht only through st+1 , hence only through (st , zta ). The deterministic

conditioned on (st , zta ) and on (st , ht ) coincide. The future-return distributions therefore agree,
and the two value functions are equal. The same induction, started from a fixed at instead of
at ∼ gθ (· | zta ), gives the action-value statement.

Step 1: V π (s, z a ) averages back to the history value. Baisero and Amato [2022] prove that V π (ht ) =
Est |ht [V π (st , ht )]. Substituting Proposition 1,
                             Est |ht [V π (st , zta )] = Est |ht [V π (st , ht )] = V π (ht ).                          (6)
The memory-state value function is therefore an unbiased estimate of the history value function.
Step 2: the two advantages coincide. Proposition 1, applied also to the action-value function, makes
the two advantages coincide pointwise on every reachable (st , ht , at ):
 Aπ (st , zta , at ) = Qπ (st , zta , at ) − V π (st , zta ) = Qπ (st , ht , at ) − V π (st , ht ) = Aπ (st , ht , at ). (7)
The two expectations are therefore taken of the same random variable, and (5) follows.

The state is essential. Both propositions rely on having the state st in the conditioning. Without it,
the policy’s memory zta alone is not a sufficient statistic for the future return. Two histories that map
to the same z a can correspond to different states, and the state in turn drives the future transitions
through T (· | st , at ). Lambrechts et al. [2025] gives a finite-time convergence analysis of asymmetric
critics with linear function approximators, in which the state appears as exactly the quantity that
eliminates aliasing in the agent state. Proposition 1 is the analogous statement, the state closes the
aliasing left by the memory.

3.4   Approximation in practice

The two value functions V π (st , ht ) and V π (st , zta ) are analytical objects. In practice, both are
approximated by neural networks trained with TD learning. The memory-state critic uses the same
architecture for the value head but reads its recurrent input from the policy’s encoder:
                                          Vψ (st , zta )   with zta = fθ (ht ).                                         (8)
The critic shares fθ in the forward pass. The critic loss is not backpropagated through fθ . The
encoder fθ is updated only by the policy gradient. A stop-gradient placed on zta in the input to
the critic implements this. Without the stop-gradient, the value loss would flow into the policy’s
encoder and shape z a to minimise value error alongside the policy loss, which is known to destabilise
on-policy training [Cobbe et al., 2021]. The resulting architecture is shown on the right of Figure 1.
By comparison, the history-state critic of Baisero and Amato [2022] is approximated by Vψ (st , ztc )
with ztc = fψ (ht ), where fψ is a critic-side recurrent encoder with parameters ψ disjoint from θ.
Both fθ and fψ are trained, the first by the policy gradient and the second by the value loss.

4     Experiments
4.1   Environment

We evaluate the memory-state critic on a vision-based pursuit-evasion environment between two
Crazyflie quadrotors. The pursuer is the only learning agent. It must get within a 20 cm radius of the
evader and within 10 seconds to neutralise it. Otherwise, the pursuer gets penalised. The arena is a
5 × 4 × 2 metre box. We use two geometries that differ in their degree of partial observability. The
open arena has no obstacles, so the evader stays visible whenever it is inside the camera frame. The
wall arena contains an opaque vertical wall with a gap, which can hide the evader from the pursuer
even when the camera points at it. The wall arena is the environment where the theory predicts the
largest gap between critics, because occlusion creates histories that the privileged state alone cannot
disambiguate.
The pursuer observes a 64 × 64 two-channel image from a camera, with a depth channel and a binary
opponent-segmentation mask, together with its previous action. A visualisation of the pursuer input is
presented in Figure 2. The policy is therefore conditioned on the history. The critic additionally sees
a 64-dimensional privileged state st with the state of both drones, relative-pose features, propeller
speeds, and an identifier of the heuristic drawn from the evader pool at the start of the episode. The
pursuer acts through a four-dimensional body-rate and collective-thrust command at 25 Hz, tracked
by an inner PD loop at 500 Hz. Dynamics are modelled at the propeller level. Implementation details
are in Appendix A.

4.2   Architecture and training

We integrate the memory-state critic into proximal policy optimization[Schulman et al., 2017], ex-
tended for recurrent actors, building on skrl[Serrano-Muñoz et al., 2023]. The actor is a convolutional
encoder followed by a GRU and an action head. It consumes the onboard camera observation and the
previous action, and outputs a continuous action distribution. The critic is a multi-layer perceptron
that takes the privileged state st concatenated with the recurrent encoded history chosen by the critic
variant. For the memory-state critic, the recurrent encoded history is the actor’s hidden state zta . For
the history-state critic, it is a critic-side encoder fψ with independent weights that generates the
hidden state ztc . Both encoders share the same architecture. For the wall arena, we use a slightly
larger discount factor γ = 0.999 to provide a longer credit-assignment horizon around the wall gap.
All other hyperparameters are unchanged. Hyperparameters are in Appendix A.

The memory-state critic outperforms the history-state critic and converges faster. Figure 3
reports the episodic return during training. In both arenas, the memory-state critic reaches at least the
final return of the history-state critic in fewer steps. This is the outcome demonstrated by Proposition 1.
In the open arena, the history-state critic plateaus lower and has way more variance across seeds. We
attribute this to its second optimisation problem, since its encoder must learn from the value loss
alone a representation predictive of future return. Therefore, it should be predictive of the actor’s next
action without observing the actor’s own representation, which is what the memory-state critic reuses
for free. While these theoretical and empirical findings are promising, they are currently limited in
terms of environment diversity. Because the construction holds for any asymmetric POMDP with a
deterministic recurrent actor, comparing it on standard POMDP benchmarks is a natural next step.
The bias of the state-only critic matters only when the state aliases the history. Propositions 1
and 2 guarantee that the critics are unbiased, but not that they beat the biased state-only critic
in every environment. The size of the bias scales with how much the privileged state aliases the
history [Lambrechts et al., 2025]. In the open arena, the evader is visible whenever it is in frame, and
the observation model is deterministic, so the state is close to Markov for the future return, and the
state-only critic is nearly unbiased. In addition, the state-only critic has an easier architecture to train.
Its return is therefore comparable to the memory-state critic’s, consistent with it solving a smaller
learning problem. In the wall arena, the evader is repeatedly occluded, so two histories that reach
the same state can disagree on where the evader was last seen. The state-only critic cannot represent
this difference, in contrast to the history-state and memory-state critic. This could explain why the
memory-state critic marginally performs better in the wall arena and the history-state critic is closer
to the other in episode return

6      Related work
Asymmetric actor-critic. Pinto et al. [2018] introduced the asymmetric actor-critic algorithm
for image-based robot learning, with a reactive policy and a state-only critic. Baisero and Amato
[2022] prove that with a history-dependent policy, the time-invariant state value function is generally
ill-defined, and that even when well-defined the resulting policy gradient is biased. They propose
the history-state critic and show that it yields an unbiased policy gradient. Lyu et al. [2022] extend
the analysis to multi-agent reinforcement learning. Lambrechts et al. [2025] give a finite-time
convergence analysis with linear function approximators. Ebi et al. [2025] generalise the construction
to partial privileged information beyond the full state. Our work provides a deep RL implementation
of the history-state critic in which the policy’s recurrent encoder is reused on the critic side, removing
the second encoder.

Recurrent policies for POMDPs. Recurrent policies are a standard way to handle partial observ-
ability, and are competitive with transformer-based variants in RL Ni et al. [2022], Hausknecht and
Stone [2015]. The history-dependent policy in our work follows this design. Our contribution is on
the critic side.

Vision-based drone control. Kaufmann et al. [2023] train a vision-based drone racing policy in
simulation with an asymmetric actor-critic, with the policy relying on state estimation from off-board
sensors at training time. Geles et al. [2024] remove the state-estimation step and train an end-to-end
vision policy with a reactive actor. The questions addressed in this work do not arise in their setting
because their policy is non-recurrent. Drone racing also exhibits little aliasing, because the next gate
rarely leaves the camera frame, in contrast to pursuit-evasion. Model-based vision RL has shown
strong results in single-agent racing [Romero et al., 2026, Verraest et al., 2025] and is complementary
to our work.

Quadrotor pursuit-evasion. Roncero et al. [2025] and Hansen et al. [2026] train quadrotor
pursuit-evasion policies under full state observation in a multi-agent setting. Bajcsy et al. [2024]


                                                                     8
consider vision-based pursuit-evasion with a ground robot evader. To the best of our knowledge,
our environment is the first open-source vision-based quadrotor pursuit-evasion with propeller-level
dynamics.

7   Conclusion
We introduced the memory-state critic, a single-encoder asymmetric critic for recurrent actors in
POMDPs. It uses the actor’s recurrent hidden state, which avoids learning a second statistic of
the history for the value function. We proved that it gives the same on-policy value function and
unbiased policy gradient as the history-state critic of Baisero and Amato [2022], with one recurrent
encoder instead of two. In a vision-based pursuit-evasion environment between two quadrotors, the
memory-state critic outperforms the history-state critic in final performance and converges faster. It
also marginally outperforms the state-only critic, especially when partial observability is important.
Sim-to-real transfer of vision-based drone policies is an active area [Yu et al., 2025], and physical
deployment is future work.

Quadrotor dynamics. We model each quadrotor as a rigid body of mass m and inertia tensor
J, with state (p, v, R, ω, Ω) for position, velocity, attitude, body rates, and the four rotor speeds.
The continuous dynamics follow Kaufmann et al. [2023]. Each rotor produces thrust kT Ω2i and
moment kM Ω2i , and the rotor speeds follow first-order dynamics Ω̇i = kmot (Ωss,i − Ωi ) towards the
commanded steady-state speed Ωss,i .

Action space and low-level control. The policy outputs a four-dimensional action a ∈ [−1, 1]4 at
25 Hz, three commanded body rates and a collective thrust. Body rates are scaled to ωkref = π ωkcmd
rad/s, and the thrust to T ref = 12 (T cmd + 1) mg ηT2W with ηT2W ≈ 3.15. An inner PD controller at
500 Hz tracks these references and produces the per-rotor setpoints.

Camera. Each drone carries a forward-facing pinhole camera rendered with IsaacLab’s tiled-
rendering pipeline, at 64 × 64 resolution and a 120◦ horizontal field of view. We read two channels,
depth and a binary opponent-segmentation mask, with a clipping range 0.02 to 20 m.

Reward and arena. The reward coefficients in (9) are R = 10.0, κa = 3.0, d0 = 0.5 m, κp = 0.02,
κρ = 1.0, and κbr = 0.001, with capture distance rc = 0.2 m and maximum episode length T = 250
steps. The arena bounds are [−2.5, 2.5] × [−2.0, 2.0] × [0.0, 2.0] m. The wall in the occluded arena
is 0.05 m thick with a 1.5 m gap, and the drone-wall clearance is 0.15 m. The body-rate weighting is
Wbr = diag(1, 1, 0.2), which lowers the yaw penalty to allow reorientation.
