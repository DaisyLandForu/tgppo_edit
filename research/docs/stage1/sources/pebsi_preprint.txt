                                      Abstract
  Mixed Integer Linear Programs (MILPs) are widely used to model real-world
  optimization problems, with the branch-and-bound (B&B) algorithm serving as
  a fundamental solution method. The choice of branching strategy is crucial, and
  recent advances in machine learning have fueled interest in learning-to-branch
  techniques. Although imitation learning (IL) has shown promising results in repli-
  cating handcrafted branching heuristics, its performance is fundamentally limited
  by the quality of the expert strategy and the high cost of data labeling. Rein-
  forcement learning (RL) presents a promising alternative, but the complexity of
  the branching process poses significant challenges for its direct application. Con-
  sequently, many existing RL-based methods still rely on expert demonstrations
  for pretraining or data augmentation, or impose additional constraints on the
  training procedure. This work introduces PEBSI, an efficient RL-based branching
  policy that eliminates the need for expert demonstrations or auxiliary configura-
  tions. The RL agent is trained using retrospective trajectories constructed from
  the original B&B search tree, with a novel reward function and exploration strat-
  egy designed to improve policy efficiency. Experimental evaluations on diverse
  MILP benchmarks demonstrate that PEBSI outperforms other RL-based meth-
  ods trained without expert guidance and, in some cases, even surpasses the
  IL-based approach.

1 Introduction
    A Mixed Integer Linear Program (MILP) is an optimization problem with a linear
objective function subject to linear constraints, where some variables are restricted
to integer values. MILPs naturally arise in a wide range of decision-making problems,
including process scheduling [1], production planning [2], vehicle routing [3], and traffic
management [4]. Solving MILPs has long been a challenge, leading to the development
of numerous algorithms, among which the branch-and-bound (B&B) algorithm [5]
stands out as a widely adopted framework. B&B systematically partitions the solution
space into a tree structure, computing relaxation bounds at each step until an optimal
integer solution is identified. Enhanced by various techniques to improve efficiency,
B&B serves as a cornerstone of modern MILP solvers [6]. The algorithm’s divide-and-
conquer nature requires making sequential decisions: at each iteration, it selects a node
from the search tree to process and branches on a variable to create subproblems. While
both node selection and variable selection influence how the search tree evolves, this
work focuses exclusively on branching variable selection, a key factor in determining
the tree’s size [7].
    Traditional variable selection rules often rely on intuition and empirical evidence
[8], with Strong Branching (SB) [9] being a notable example that typically produces
smaller search trees than other expert-crafted rules at the cost of extensive computa-
tion. More recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a compelling alternative,
enabling the development of data-driven branching strategies. This has led to a well-
established body of work [10–15] that employs imitation learning (IL) to approximate
the decision-making process of SB. However, no universally optimal branching pol-
icy exists for all problem types, and SB is not always the best choice. It prioritizes
local one-step look-ahead dual-bound improvements, which can sometimes lead to
suboptimal decisions by neglecting the global impact on deeper levels of the B&B
tree [16]. Additionally, it may struggle to provide reliable demonstrations when the
linear programming (LP) relaxation is uninformative or suffers from dual degener-
acy [17, 18]. The limitations of the expert strategy can hinder the effectiveness of the
learned IL policy. Furthermore, obtaining IL training data is non-trivial due to the
high computational cost of executing the expert strategy.
    Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising approach to address these
challenges, offering the potential to derive effective branching strategies without expert
supervision. However, training an RL branching policy from scratch remains diffi-
cult in practice due to long episode durations, a large state-action space, and partial
observability [19]. As a result, most existing studies still rely on expert demonstra-
tions for model pretraining or sample augmentation [20–24]. Etheve et al. [25] were
the first to propose an RL-based branching policy learned without expert guidance,
but their approach requires using a specific, suboptimal node selection rule during
training. Scavuzzo et al. [26] extended their work and proposed a different method to
eliminate the reliance on expert experience, but it requires solving training instances
in advance. These additional restrictions are often impractical in real-world settings.
    This work presents PEBSI, a novel RL-based approach for learning a variable
selection strategy within the B&B framework that removes the need for expert
demonstrations or additional constraints during training. Building on the insights of


                                            2
Parsonson et al. [19], the RL agent is trained by learning from retrospectively con-
structed trajectories of the full B&B search tree. A carefully designed reward function,
along with an exploration strategy, is proposed to enhance performance. Experimen-
tal results demonstrate PEBSI’s superior performance in policy efficiency over other
RL-based methods trained from scratch [19, 25, 26]. In some cases, it even exceeds
the IL-based method [13]. In summary, the contributions of this work are threefold:
• Propose an efficient RL-based branching policy without requiring expert demon-
  strations or auxiliary training settings.
• Propose a reward function to capture the varying impact of agent actions and
  encourage effective decisions during solving.
• Propose an exploration strategy to reduce uninformative trajectories and improve
  the quality of training samples.

2 Background
2.1 The B&B Algorithm
   A general MILP can be defined as

                          min{cT x : Ax ≤ b, x ∈ Zk × Rn−k },                             (1)

where c ∈ Rn , A ∈ Rm×n , b ∈ Rm , cT x is the objective function, and k ≤ n is the
number of integer variables. B&B is a classic tree search algorithm that iteratively
partitions the feasible set of the original problem (1) into disjoint subproblems and
computes bounds for these subproblems to rule out unpromising regions.
    The algorithm maintains a list of unexplored leaf nodes, known as open nodes,
initialized with the root node representing the original problem. At each iteration,
B&B selects an open node based on a predefined strategy, a decision process referred to
as the node selection problem. Once a node is selected, the algorithm relaxes the integer
constraints and solves the resulting LP relaxation, a convex optimization problem
[27]. The optimal solution, denoted as x∗ , provides a lower bound (also known as dual
bound ) for the original problem. If x∗ also satisfies the integer constraints, it constitutes
a feasible solution and provides an upper bound. Otherwise, the fractional variables in
x∗ , also called candidates, are used to further partition the search space. The variable
selection problem consists of choosing a candidate xj ∈ x∗ (j ∈ {1, . . . , k}) to branch
on, creating child nodes according to the split

                                 xj ≤ ⌊x∗ j ⌋ ∨ xj ≥ ⌈x∗ j ⌉.                             (2)

If the lower bound of a node exceeds the objective value of the current best feasible
solution, the bounding mechanism prunes the node, as no solution within its subtree
can be optimal. An example illustrating the application of B&B to solve an MILP
instance is provided in Figure 1. This work focuses on the critical task of variable
selection, as the quality of the branching strategy dramatically influences the search
process and the overall efficiency of B&B.

2.2 Traditional branching heuristics
    While there is no universal metric to measure the efficiency of a branching policy,
a common evaluation criterion is the final size of the B&B tree [28], as it reflects
the policy’s decision-making quality at each step. The literature offers a variety of
branching rules, with SB empirically known to produce significantly smaller search
trees than other heuristics. However, SB achieves this by calculating the expected
one-step dual bound improvement for each candidate before branching, which can be
myopic, as it neglects long-term effects on deeper levels of the tree, potentially leading
to suboptimal outcomes [16]. Moreover, when the LP relaxation is uninformative or
exhibits dual degeneracy (i.e., multiple simplex bases yield the same dual solution)
[17, 18], SB may fail to provide a meaningful selection criterion. Its high computational
cost further limits practical deployment at every node.
    In practice, solvers employ more efficient branching strategies. One such method
is pseudo-cost branching (PC) [29], which tracks the impact of past branchings on LP
relaxation values for each variable. With sufficient historical data, PC can approximate
SB’s gains using only a few arithmetic operations. Despite its efficiency, PC’s per-
formance is highly sensitive to proper initialization. Recent advances, such as hybrid


                                                                4
branching [30], combine the strengths of SB and PC by applying SB in the early
stages and later switching to faster heuristics like PC. For a comprehensive review of
traditional branching rules, readers are referred to [16].

2.3 Markov decision process (MDP) formulation
    RL is typically formalized through the framework of MDP, which can be defined by
(S, A, T , R, γ ). Here, S represents the state space, A the action space, T the transition
function, R the reward function, and γ ∈ [0, 1] the discount factor. At each time step
t, selecting an action at ∈ A transitions the system from st ∈ S to st+1 ∈ S according
to T , yielding a reward rt based on R. The discount factor γ balances immediate
rewards and long-term gains. Given a training dataset D consisting of samples in the
form (st , at , st+1 , rt ), the RL agent learns a policy π using specific algorithms.
    In the context of branching variable selection, the MDP state encodes the current
status of the search tree, often represented as a bipartite graph [13]. The action space
consists of candidate variables, and selecting an action corresponds to choosing a
variable to branch on. State transitions are determined by the solver’s node selection
rule, which is separate from variable selection, making the transitions only partially
observable to the brancher. Details on the reward function design of this work are
provided in Section 4.

3 Related works
    Developing an effective branching strategy is essential for enhancing the perfor-
mance of modern MILP solvers. By leveraging the vast amount of data generated
during the optimization process, learn-to-branch methods have gained significant
attention, with using IL to mimic the behavior of SB emerging as the most extensively
studied approach. Early studies in this area employed fixed-dimensional representa-
tions [10–12]. Gasse et al. [13] later advanced this approach by introducing a bipartite
graph representation and a policy model based on Graph Convolutional Networks
(GCNs). Building on their work, subsequent research further expanded the field
[14, 15, 31]. However, as previously discussed, the effectiveness of IL is inherently
constrained by the expert strategy and requires costly data labeling. RL approaches
are recognized for the ability to learn an optimal policy directly with respect to any
desired metric, driving growing interest in their application to this field.
    Sun et al. [20] proposed a primal-dual policy network (PD policy) and trained
the RL agent using a novelty search evolution strategy. They initialized the policy in
the same way as [13] with less training data, otherwise the model can hardly solve
an instance in a reasonable time. Qu et al. [21] also employed a supervised learn-
ing approach as a warm start for their model. The pretrained model can be used to
produce informative self-generated samples, which were mixed with expert demonstra-
tions for policy learning. Zhang et al. [22], on the other hand, proposed an algorithm
called HRL-Aug, which mixes the samples adaptively and dynamically using an online
RL agent. Huang et al. [24] highlighted the importance of optimizing the utility of
the entire MILP solving process rather than focusing narrowly on individual steps.
They proposed Branch Ranking (BR), formulated the branching task as an offline RL


                                            5
problem, constructing a dataset that combined samples from SB demonstrations with
those from rollout trajectories (referred to as short-term and long-term information,
respectively). Similarly, Zhang et al. [23] emphasized the utility of the search tree
by incorporating Monte-Carlo tree search (MCTS) [32] in policy optimization. They
proposed PPO-MCTS, which also used IL to pretrain the RL agent. Although these
studies have explored RL to improve performance, they still rely on expert demon-
strations for pretraining or data augmentation, missing RL’s long-term promise of
learning novel policies independently from human-designed guidance and potentially
surpassing expert experience.
    Etheve et al. [25] were the first to learn a branching policy without expert guidance.
They proposed an algorithm called FMSTS, demonstrating that under a depth-first
search (DFS) node selection rule [33], minimizing the size of each subtree is equivalent
to minimizing the size of the whole tree. Building on this insight, they developed a
value-based RL procedure to learn the policy. However, pure DFS can be significantly
less effective than the default node selection strategy used in most MILP solvers [29].
Scavuzzo et al. [26] revisited this approach and proposed a framework termed tree
Markov Decision Processes (tMDP). They argued that enforcing DFS for node selec-
tion and providing the optimal objective limit during training can both trigger the
tree Markov property, enabling the use of policy gradient methods for optimization.
However, similar to the DFS requirement, the need for presolving training instances
remains impractical in real-world applications. Parsonson et al. [19] proposed retro
branching (Retro), which learns from retrospective trajectories of the search tree using
Deep Q-Network (DQN) [34], eliminating additional restrictions on the training pro-
cedure. However, their approach assigns equal credit to all steps that do not lead to a
fathomed node, overlooking the varying impact of different actions. Furthermore, their
reliance on simple stochastic action sampling for exploration may struggle to effec-
tively navigate the vast state-action space and complex sequential decision-making
inherent in branching.
    The related RL-based approaches are summarized in Table 1. The proposed PEBSI
learns without relying on expert demonstrations or auxiliary training settings. It cap-
tures the intrinsic impact of branching decisions on the solving process through a
tailored reward function. It also employs a novel exploration strategy that filters out
uninformative trajectories and enhances the quality of training samples.

4 Methodology
4.1 Retrospective trajectories
    Applying RL directly from scratch can be computationally intractable considering
the exponential size of the entire search tree. To address this challenge, the approach of
learning from retrospectively constructed trajectories [19] is adopted. This paradigm
offers a general framework compatible with various RL algorithms. An illustration of
the pipeline is presented in Figure 2.
    The RL brancher is first applied to the training instance to generate an ini-
tial episode, forming the original search tree. After solving the instance, rather than

directly using the observed MDP transitions for model optimization, multiple retro-
spective trajectories are constructed from the original tree. This construction process
iteratively links the highest-level available node (i.e., not yet assigned to any trajec-
tory) to an unselected fathomed leaf within its subtree, ensuring that each node is
included in exactly one trajectory. The transitions within each retrospective trajectory
are then utilized for learning.
    This approach eliminates the need for using DFS node selection strategy or presolv-
ing the instances during training. By restricting sequential nodes to the same subtree,
it also enhances observability, as the characteristic changes in the MDP states become
more closely linked to the branching actions. Furthermore, the episode length is sig-
nificantly reduced compared to the size of the full tree, enabling the agent to learn
effectively without expert guidance.
    Following [13], the states are encoded using the classic bipartite graph represen-
tation, and the network architecture consists of a series of GCN modules, followed
by a final Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) module. The processed embeddings are then
passed through a softmax activation function to produce a probability distribution
over the candidates.

4.3 Return and explore
    Given the vast state-action space and the complexity of sequential decision-making
in B&B, relying solely on random actions for exploration may be insufficient to yield
promising results [35], particularly when learning from scratch. Therefore, an efficient
RL branching policy requires a well-designed exploration strategy to identify high-
quality training samples. Revisiting Figure 3(a), the trajectory from node 22 to node
27 fails to improve the dual bound in any way. Such trajectories are deemed undesir-
able, potentially generating a substantial number of samples that are not beneficial
for learning an effective policy. To facilitate dual bound improvement and efficiently
complete the optimization, the exploration strategy in PEBSI focuses on refining the
quality of retrospective trajectories, allowing the agent to acquire more informative
training samples.
    Consider an undesirable trajectory, the procedure of the strategy is presented in
Algorithm 1. It first returns to the starting node of the trajectory, randomly selects
different actions at that step, and then finishes the corresponding solving processes
again. This generates new subtrees originating from the starting node, enabling the
reconstruction of new trajectories. If these new trajectories can improve the dual bound
or even terminate the optimization, they are more likely to provide valuable insights
for the agent. The trajectory that achieves the greatest dual bound improvement is

strategy to the undesirable trajectory in Figure 3(a).

5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental setup
5.1.1 Benchmarks
    Following common benchmark selection practices in the research community
[19, 20, 22–24, 26], PEBSI is evaluated on four classic combinatorial optimization prob-
lems: set covering (Setcover), capacitated facility location (Facility),
maximum independent set (Indset), and multiple knapsack (Mknapsack).
These benchmarks vary in constraint structures, the presence of continuous variables
and non-binary integer variables, and optimization directions [37]. They are widely
used to model real-world problems [8], making them representative for this study.
    For training, a total of 10,000 instances are generated. The policy is then evaluated
on two datasets: a test set of 100 instances matching the training problem size (same
number of variables and constraints) and a transfer set of 100 instances with a larger
problem size (more variables and constraints). Detailed benchmark parameter settings
are provided in Appendix A.

5.1.2 Baselines
   PEBSI is evaluated against three RL-based baselines that do not rely on expert
demonstrations: FMSTS [25], tMDP [26], and Retro [19]. For tMDP, its tree MDP
property is activated by providing the optimal objective limit during training.
Additionally, for reference, the performance of the IL-based policy trained with
SB demonstrations [13] is also reported. All experimental configurations are kept
consistent with those specified in the respective papers.

5.1.3 Training and evaluation
    PEBSI does not impose specific requirements on the node selection rule during
training, and the default solver setting for node selection is maintained in this study. As
noted in [19], the choice of construction heuristic has little impact on the performance
of the RL agent. In this study, the leaf nodes of the retrospective trajectories are
selected randomly. The exploration strategy outlined in Algorithm 1 is applied during
the initial 20,000 training epochs, with the number of different actions trials set to 10.

    The back-end solver is SCIP [38], one of the fastest open-source MILP solvers.

[41], and Ecole [42] libraries. All experiments are performed on a machine equipped
with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6348 CPU running at 2.60 GHz and an Nvidia Tesla
V100S-PCIE-32GB GPU.

5.2.1 Comparative results
    Table 3 presents the solving performance of each branching policy across the
benchmarks. PEBSI consistently achieves smaller search trees than other RL baselines
in nearly all cases and performs comparably to the best method when not leading.
While RL-based approaches still lag behind the IL-based method that leverages expert
demonstrations, notable exceptions arise. For Facility, PEBSI matches and even
slightly outperforms the IL approach on the test set. Additionally, for Mknapsack,
RL-based branching policies surpass IL in most cases, potentially due to the distinct
characteristics of different MILPs. Specifically, as previously discussed, the poor LP


                                             12
relaxation provided by the problem formulation often results in little or no dual bound
improvement after branching. This diminishes the informativeness of SB scores, which
IL heavily relies on, thereby limiting its performance. These observations highlight
the potential of RL as a viable alternative to IL for discovering superior branching
strategies across diverse MILPs. It is also noted that Retro exhibits convergence dif-
ficulties on Indset. A possible explanation lies in its algorithmic structure (off-policy
training combined with function approximation and bootstrapping), which can lead
to instability and divergence for certain tasks [32].

5.2.3 Informative features
    To gain further insight, a qualitative analysis of variable feature importance in
branching decisions was conducted, using the gradients of the output score with respect
to the input features. The variable features are grouped based on the type of infor-
mation they convey, as summarized in Table 5. Details of the features are provided
in Appendix B. To analyze the dynamics of feature importance during solving, each
benchmark was examined with respect to changes across branching steps. Consis-
tent patterns were observed among instances within the same problem set. Figure 6
illustrates one representative instance per benchmark. The results show that fea-
ture importance evolves during solving, and different problem types exhibit distinct
characteristics.
    In Setcover, the objective coefficient (f1 ) is initially influential. As solving pro-
gresses, features associated with the LP solution value and its fractionality (f7 , f8 )
become more dominant. In Facility and Indset, LP solutions are also critical. In
addition, features that capture structural information, such as number of leaf nodes
(f24 ) and current node depth (f32 ), can also be important. In Mknapsack, uninforma-
tive LP-related features tend to be less important, while information about feasible


                                                                        15
solutions (f28 , f29 ) plays a more prominent role. This observation is consistent with
the design choice of PEBSI to avoid reliance on SB scores and leverage global bound
improvement signals to refine the search process.
    Overall, the analysis highlights the significance of learning a branching policy
that adapts to problem-specific characteristics and incorporates diverse information
throughout the solving process.

strategy, highlighting their contributions to performance improvement. The analysis
